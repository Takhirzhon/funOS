# La La Land, Past Lives, and the endings that are right

Flying to Copenhagen for the weekend. Last night I watched *Past Lives*, and it left the same residue as *La La Land* did last summer. Two films with the same kind of ending - the two people understand they cannot be together - and both of them are right about it. I did not know how to describe the feeling. A strange, sharp pain in the chest. So, both films, in the order I saw them.

## La La Land

![La La Land](/portfolio/My%20Documents/My%20Blog/Pictures/La%20La%20Land,%20poster.png)

I watched it seven years after it came out, in July 2023, and it was the film of that year for me. Two people go through the hard part together. At the end, when they talk about what will happen to them, Sebastian says time will put everything in its place and they will find out. She becomes a star and finds happiness with another man; he lives alone and opens the bar he wanted so badly. In the last scene they look at each other and nod - each saying that they are glad, that they accept what happened in the other's life, that each of them is happy, even with the feelings still there.

The lesson I took: your love should hold you up and push you to act. They helped each other become who they are, and then the roads split - film and jazz. They could not be together, and at the end they tell each other, without a word, that it is all right. It is on their faces.

## Past Lives

![Past Lives](/portfolio/My%20Documents/My%20Blog/Pictures/Past%20Lives,%20poster.png)

The same ending, and completely different. What is interesting is that the heroes of the two films come to the same place from different pasts. In *La La Land* two people build each other and outgrow the thing they built. In *Past Lives* two people were twelve together, and then one of them left - and twenty-four years later they stand on a street in New York and find out whether the thing between them was a life or a past life.

She has a husband, and the husband is a good man, and the film never once pretends otherwise. That is the part that hurt. There is no villain to blame. There is only the fact that a person can be the right one and the timing can still be wrong, and that the right thing is to stand at the corner, say goodbye, and walk back to the door where someone is waiting for you.

## And Green Book, for the opposite

![Green Book](/portfolio/My%20Documents/My%20Blog/Pictures/Green%20Book,%20poster.png)

I put *Green Book* next to these two because it ends the other way: two people who should not have anything in common end up at the same table on Christmas Eve, and stay in each other's lives. It is not a love story, so the comparison is not fair. But it is the same question - what do two people owe each other after the road ends - with the other answer. A bouncer from the Bronx and a pianist with three doctorates drive through the South for two months, and the film is honest enough that the pianist teaches the bouncer to write a letter and the bouncer teaches the pianist to eat fried chicken with his hands, and neither lesson is the small one.

![Green Book, the clip](/portfolio/My%20Documents/My%20Blog/Pictures/Green%20Book,%20the%20clip.jpg)

The scene: [Green Book on YouTube](https://youtu.be/3sz7V02xp5s).

Three films. Two say: let go, and mean it. One says: don't. I think all three are right, which is the uncomfortable part.
