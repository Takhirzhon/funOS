# White Nights, an angry review

![Dostoevsky, White Nights, 1865 edition](/portfolio/My%20Documents/My%20Blog/Pictures/White%20Nights,%201865%20edition.jpg)

Coming back from Issyk-Kul, passing the village of Ottuk. A good trip - the road wears you out, but the views calm you down again. To pass the time in the car I read Dostoevsky's *White Nights*, on a friend's recommendation. It may be Dostoevsky, but the narrator can go to hell.

Spoilers, obviously.

The narrator is a dreamer who thinks he can build something with a girl who has spent a year waiting for a man who promised to earn some money and come back for her. They meet on four white nights in a row. On the first two they tell each other their life stories. On the third he helps her deliver a letter. On the fourth she says the man she is waiting for still has not written - even though, as she mentioned on day two, he is already back in the city.

And this is where the narrator uses the fact that she is stressed and crying to announce that he loves her beyond measure. She answers: *if you were him, I would love you back.* Dude. They do not want you there. He talks it around until she believes she really does love him now, despite saying more than once over the first three days that she loves him like a brother. They make plans: he will move in with her (she lives with her grandmother, who lets a room, and he wants to be there). It is nearly morning. He walks her home, and a man passes them, turns, and says: *Nastenka...*

She runs and hugs her man, comes back, kisses the narrator hard, says goodbye. Then she writes him a letter, asks his forgiveness, and invites him to the wedding: *I will love you to the end of my days all the same, you are not indifferent to me.*

Right.

There are good things in it, to be fair:

- Why the person who recommended it recommended it. (No comment.)
- Best, or beloved? Nastenka answered *beloved*, which is the logical answer.
- Dreaming is fine, but dreamers hit reality harder than most and can do real damage on the way down. After he reads the letter, the narrator - the whole story is his - says that everything went grey, and that he does not understand how he lived in such a terrible room, or how his cleaning woman got so old.

That last page is the book.
