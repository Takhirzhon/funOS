# The Burnout Society, chapter by chapter

![Byung-Chul Han, The Burnout Society](/portfolio/My%20Documents/My%20Blog/Pictures/The%20Burnout%20Society,%20cover.jpg)

Read Byung-Chul Han's *The Burnout Society* this week. It is short - an evening, if you do not stop to write in the margins. I stopped a lot. These are the margins, collected so I can come back to them.

## Neuronal violence

Han opens with the idea that every age has its signature disease. It used to be viruses and bacteria - something foreign that attacks from outside, and you defend. In the 21st century the signature diseases are neuronal: depression, ADHD, burnout. Nobody is attacking. The person is overloaded by what is asked of him - be active, be productive, be engaged, be capable, be efficient. Violence today is not physical and not institutional (hello, Foucault). It is internal. His name for the cause: an excess of positivity.

## Beyond the disciplinary society

We no longer live in Foucault's society of control, built on institutions and prohibitions. We live in an achievement society. Control took people to a certain ceiling and stopped; to raise output past it, the society itself had to change. So now it says: *you can*, *there are no limits*, *if he did it, so can you*. The person must achieve, constantly.

There is a parallel with the Stakhanovite movement in the USSR - a cult of achievement, of tempo, of hero-workers. The difference is that the pressure is no longer external. If something goes wrong, you are guilty, not the system. The control is built into the person.

## Deep boredom

This chapter is about hyperactive attention. The modern person is always switching, reacting, looking, scrolling, reading notifications. He looks attentive, but he cannot stay on anything. What is missing is *contemplative* attention - the ability to be with one thing for a long time, in silence, without a goal. The ego is hyperactive but shallow, and the space for depth, reflection and real creativity disappears.

Han writes that concern for the good life gives way to concern for survival. You live in tension, not in peace; you keep yourself afloat. Nietzsche wrote that restless people have never had as many opportunities as they do now.

My question in the margin: aren't the restless people exactly the ones who move society forward? They start businesses, projects, the economy. So the achievement society does produce something. The question is the price - and I think the price is health and happiness, most of the time.

## Vita activa

Frantic activity has pushed out contemplation (*vita contemplativa*). Man stopped being a thinking creature and became *bestia laborans* - the working animal. We are always busy; even resting, we think "I will rest properly so I can work harder". We do a lot and think little about the meaning of it. The result is that we do not live, we function.

## The pedagogy of seeing

Hyperactivity makes a society tired. Everyone is developing, improving, running, doing. That activity produces exhaustion, and exhaustion produces passivity. And all this positivity - the hyperactivity, the "successful success" - pushes negative feelings out. You may not be angry, may not hate, may not be irritated; people demand that you be motivated, positive and proactive. But the rage is still in there. It is not spent.

Maybe that is why people hate bloggers and successful people so much. They become a mirror of your own failure. You compare, feel your inadequacy, and the anger comes out of that. Hate is just a way to release the rage that had nowhere to go.

## The Bartleby case

You need to have read Melville's *Bartleby, the Scrivener* for this one; I read a summary. Han uses the whole plot and the character as a picture of what is happening today - a man who steps out of the achievement society without a protest, by drawing a line: *I would prefer not to*.

## The burnout society

The conclusion: we are becoming a doping society. To be better still, more productive still, we enhance ourselves - preparations, stimulants, biohacking. A person becomes a project of endless improvement, so he can serve society with his mind, speed, strength and efficiency. The organism was not built for this. Han writes that modern tiredness is a *lonely* tiredness: we think the problem is in us, so we isolate ourselves to fix it, to recover, to clear our heads of what everyone close to us hopes for and wants from us. My note on the last chapter: constant work for success separates people.

## What I took from it

I liked the ideas. Leaving this society is close to impossible. But you can understand how it works and use it - toward your own ends, with the system and the wants of the people who keep improving it working for you rather than on you.
